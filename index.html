Explainer · Bundestagswahl 2025

How does an election forecast model work?

Chirag Ramesh  ·  Data Journalism Final Project  ·  Hertie School 2026

You've read the headlines talking about where the polls are at right now and who is leading them. You may have also read about an election forecasting model predicting a certain party to win the elections. But have you wondered what may be happening underneath? Did you think polls and election forecasting models are the same thing?

When scientists build models, they mean to say that they're trying to predict something based on some patterns observed. It is basically a formula that takes inputs and generates an output. Inputs can be anything from people's salaries, a constituency's education level, or a region's unemployment rate. The output is what we're trying to predict, and it could be anything from people's likelihood of buying a car, whether a region will see rainfall, or in the case of election forecasting models, probability of a party's vote share or a candidate's chances of winning a constituency. The result is what we call a prediction, and there are nuances to these results, but more on that in a moment.

Polls and election forecasting models are not the same thing, and whoever makes them are not entirely certain of the results either. At Zweitstimme, we predict the results of both the vote shares of parties of Zweitstimme and constituency-level candidate win probabilities of Erststimme. We do this with two models, one for each. A poll tells us that if, for whatever reason, an election were to be conducted, say next Sunday, what would the results look like? It is a snapshot at a given time, and they do this by surveying people across the country by asking people a simple question, "Who would you vote for?". Polls are conducted throughout the year, even during years with no election scheduled.

Part I · The Zweitstimme Model

Building a forecast, ingredient by ingredient

The Zweitstimme model combines what history tells us with what current polls say to make predictions about the upcoming election. It's not just a snapshot, it's trying to say that while polls are saying X, there are historical patterns which tell us how the results are probably going to be different or sometimes, it might just confirm what the polls are showing. The model shows different results when checked four months prior to the election, versus just two days before the election.

Structural component · Step 0

What the 2021 result tells us.

The most recent election result, 2021, is the starting point the model builds on. Each party's line begins in 2017 and ends in 2021 to reflect how vote shares changed between the two most recent elections.

Structural component · Step 1

What happens if the trend continues.

To illustrate why more inputs are needed, if we simply extended the 2017 to 2021 trend forward, it would predict SPD as the clear winner with a vote share above 30%. But we know that's nowhere close to the truth. Hence the model will need other inputs to make a better prediction.

Note: While only 2017 and 2021 vote shares are used for describing how historical patterns are included, in reality, data of party vote share since 1949 elections are fed into the model.

Polling component · Step 2

What polls are saying now.

The model also needs current trends, so a simple average of all polls conducted by different institutes between Oct 2024–Feb 2025 are added into the model. We are already closer to the actual result now.

Polling component · Step 3

Not all institutes are equal.

Unfortunately different institutes have historically shown systematic tendencies to over- or under-report certain parties' vote shares. This is called a house effect. Click on the different parties below to see how INSA runs high for AfD, while Forschungsgruppe runs low. These house effects need to be corrected, so another input in the model is the house effect corrections. So now do we have the forecast?

House effect = institute average minus field average. Positive = runs high.

Uncertainty · 4 months prior

The forecast is a range, not a number.

Not yet, these forecasts are not certain. The model runs thousands of simulations to arrive at these forecasted vote shares. Four months prior to the election, CDU's estimate shows 30.2%, the model is saying that of the many simulations run, CDU's vote share of 30.2% is the result achieved 1 of 6 times. Some of the simulations showed results of ~35% and some even ~25%, this is the confidence interval which describes the uncertainty component of the model. The predictions of ~35% and ~25% happen rarely, when compared to 30.2%, which appears 1 in 6 times.

Also, notice the size of the I bar, that is how much uncertainty exists with the result of 30.2% four months prior to the election, because at this time, the model considers the historical results more seriously than the latest polls.

Uncertainty · 3 months prior

More polls, narrower range.

3 months out: Intervals begin to narrow slightly as more polls arrive, more weight is given to the polls now than the historical patterns.

Uncertainty · 1 month prior

Something unusual is happening.

1 month out: Linke's point estimate is rising but the interval is not shrinking. Something unusual is happening here. That's because it reflects the truth. Die Linke's popularity rose sharply in the weeks before the election, but the polls disagreed with each other about how much, so the model's uncertainty grew alongside the rising estimate.

Uncertainty · Final prediction

The final picture.

The final predictions are released 2 days before the election. Notice how BSW's interval halved, Linke's widened. Die Linke, FDP and BSW straddle the 5% line. Let's take a closer look at what happened here.

The 5% threshold

Final prediction, 2 days before election. I-bars = 83% CI. ★ = actual 2025 result. Source: Zweitstimme forecast API, Jan 2025.

Who was at risk of disappearing.

Since 5% is the threshold for entering parliament, its important to see what happened here. FDP fell to 4.3%. BSW scraped through at 4.97%. Linke surged to 8.8%. So was the model wrong? No, it was correct, because the model reported confidence intervals along with the point estimates for each party's vote share. Even the unexpected 8.8% result of Die Linke was within the 8.9% upper limit of the confidence interval!

Zweitstimme gives national vote shares, but that's not the end of the model's work. Nearly half of Bundestag seats come from 299 direct constituency races. And the two are connected: a party that falls below 5% can still enter parliament if it wins at least three direct constituencies, the Grundmandatsklausel. The Erststimme model predicts those constituency races. Together, the two models produce parliament entry probabilities and coalition majority probabilities.

Also an important note on the explainer so far, the explainer is only a simplified version of what the actual model does, where more complex techniques such as a joint Bayesian framework combining historical and polling components, estimated using Stan (Stoetzer et al. 2019) are used to predict vote share.

Part II · The Erststimme Model

Predicting 299 constituency races

Building the Erststimme model is fundamentally different because no local polls exist for individual constituencies. The Erststimme model instead builds up from the national Zweitstimme forecast, applies it proportionally to each constituency, then adjusts for local and candidate-level factors. The output is a win probability across thousands of simulations. Scroll through two contrasting cases, one where the model worked, and one where it did not.

Freiburg (WK 281) · Constituency & candidate factors

Local knowledge changes everything.

Adding constituency and candidate factors: Freiburg is a university city with a strong Grüne tradition and a well-known incumbent candidate. These factors include whether the incumbent is running, the candidate's list position, prior candidacy experience, gender, academic title, number of candidates in the constituency, and the Zweitstimme forecast for that constituency. The full model pushes Grüne to 32.8%, win probability 94%. The filled dot shows the point estimate, the hollow circle shows the actual 2025 result. The model predicted the right winner, but overshot the margin. (Neunhoeffer et al. 2020, Politische Vierteljahresschrift)

Freiburg (WK 281) · Uncertainty

How confident was the model.

The I-bars show the 83% credibility interval. Grüne's interval [29.6–35.8%] sits well above CDU/CSU's [20.4–25.7%]. The model was confident Grüne would win, and it did. But the actual result of 29.0% fell just outside the lower bound. The model correctly predicted the winner, but was overconfident about the margin.

Berlin-Neukölln (WK 81) · Proportional swing

The national collapse hits hard.

The national SPD collapse pushes its Neukölln estimate to 14.2%. CDU/CSU rises to 20.9% and takes the lead. AfD surges to 15.3%. The race suddenly looks wide open between three parties.

Berlin-Neukölln (WK 81) · Constituency & candidate factors

A coin flip prediction.

The full model gives CDU/CSU 20.5% versus Grüne 20.3%, essentially a coin flip. Win probabilities: CDU/CSU 45%, Grüne 40%, Linke 0%. The filled dot shows the point estimate, the hollow circle shows what actually happened. Linke won with ~26%. Neukölln is a traditionally left district. The Left party campaigned effectively into a void left by other parties, particularly the Greens, moving away from traditional left positions. That kind of campaign dynamic, voters actively shifting in response to other parties' positioning, is what no model can capture in advance.

Berlin-Neukölln (WK 81) · Uncertainty

The model missed what it couldn't see.

CDU/CSU [17.9–22.9%] and Grüne [17.7–22.6%] have almost identical intervals, the model honestly could not call this race. But Linke, the actual winner, had a 0% win probability. The model's uncertainty was real, but it was uncertain about the wrong candidates. This is the honest limit of any statistical model: it cannot predict what it cannot measure.

Conclusion

The Zweitstimme and Erststimme models work as a system, where the vote share forecasts from Zweitstimme model feed into parliament entry probabilities, accounting for both the 5% threshold and the Grundmandatsklausel. Constituency predictions then feed into coalition majority probability calculations, which is another thing we predict at Zweitstimme. The model got 270 of 299 constituencies right, and in the remaining 29, personal vote factors that no model can measure made the difference. The credibility intervals told you in advance the model did not know. That honesty is a feature of the election forecasting model, not a flaw. A forecast that admits uncertainty is more useful than one that claims false certainty. Understanding these nuances makes all the difference between reading a headline and understanding what it actually means. Read the full evaluation of the 2025 forecast at zweitstimme.org.
